The Second Chance Pathway Planner

Connecticut’s Clean Slate law has erased the convictions of 170,000 people. While this law was meant to give people a fresh start, the state has struggled to notify the people whose convictions were erased. The Second Chance Pathway Planner works for individuals or caseworkers to check records, build 30-day plans, and practice conversations that matter.

The problem

The law already helped her. Nobody told her.

Connecticut’s Clean Slate law (2021) was meant to give people a fresh start, but the state has no system to tell the 170,000 people whose convictions it erased, so many still turn down jobs and apartments over records that no longer exist. Women are hit especially hard: nearly half of the women at York Correctional Institution, Connecticut’s only women’s prison, are mothers.

Why should we care?

Connecticut has already spent the money and passed the law. The state is investing $16.6 million to implement Clean Slate. The benefit is there, but it isn’t reaching the people it was built for. That’s where The Second Chance Pathway Planner comes in.

Sources: CT Mirror, July 15, 2026; Connecticut Public, Dec. 26, 2025; CT Capitol Dispatch, Mar. 18, 2026; NBC Connecticut, June 27, 2023; Prison Policy Initiative, 2018.
Connecticut’s Clean Slate law (Public Act No. 21-32) was passed in May 2021 and signed into law in June 2021. While this law was meant to give people a fresh start by erasing convictions for 170,000 people, technology problems have delayed notifying these individuals. That means that many formerly incarcerated individuals are turning down jobs or apartments or checking “yes” on a conviction box over a record that no longer legally exists. This gap hits formerly incarcerated women especially hard: nearly half of the women at York Correctional Institution, Connecticut’s only women’s prison, are mothers. When a woman comes home, she is often trying to find work, secure housing, and reunify with her children all at once and many are doing it without a caseworker. Personal tie: my father-in-law, Governor Dan Malloy, started this work, and I served on the board of the Women’s Prison Association.

The closest competitors are AI reentry planners like Next Steps in North Carolina. None uses Connecticut law, none checks Clean Slate status, and none is built around women. The biggest difference from ChatGPT: eligibility is calculated in plain Python from the state's rules, and the AI only explains the result.
How the agents work

The app uses eight AI agents plus one part that is not AI.

The part that is not AI is the Clean Slate rules engine, written in plain Python. The agents don’t decide among themselves who works next. A short Python program (the orchestrator) hands the work along in a fixed order. Every run takes the same path, so the cost is predictable, each step can be tested on its own, and agents can’t pass work back and forth forever.

Key design choices

The rules engine decides each conviction’s result; the screener can’t change it.

The planner’s tools search 41 checked CT resources and find the nearest Reentry Welcome Center.

The verifier checks every plan and sends it back once if needed.

The Practice Room agents never see her convictions.

All eight agents use gpt-6-luna through Portkey.

Safety guard first, then intake turns answers or notes into a profile, then the rules engine. The screener explains the result, the planner builds the plan with tools, and the verifier checks it. In the Practice Room, the role-play partner, coach, and summary never see her convictions.
Demo 1 · Planning for herself

A formerly convicted woman checks her record

Judith lives in Hartford, has two children, and a 2009 drug possession conviction. She isn’t sure if it was a misdemeanor or a felony. She uses the app to check her record. The run took about a minute and cost about half a cent ($0.0055).

Logged run on the live code, November 9, 2026 (session 5bc6a3b2): 10 model calls; 34,626 input tokens (11,353 cached) + 6,077 output = 40,703 tokens, $0.0055.
12 questions, then her result and next steps (official record first), then her goals, then a 4-week plan with checked contacts. The app says her conviction needs more information and tells her exactly how to get her official record. The verifier sent the first plan back once, which is why this run cost more than the $0.0037 median.
Demo 2 · A caseworker

A caseworker checks his client’s record

Tauhid is a caseworker in New Haven, CT. He doesn’t have to sort the facts about his clients into a form. Instead, he uses the app to paste his case notes as they are, and the intake agent pulls out what the rules engine needs. These notes are for a sample client with three convictions, current probation, and an open DCF case. Before Submit, a free checklist in code lists facts the notes leave out. The run took about 35 seconds and cost less than half a cent ($0.0045).

Logged run, November 9, 2026 (session 53477090): 6 model calls; 20,523 input tokens (1,351 cached) + 5,104 output = 25,627 tokens, $0.0045.
Caseworkers also see the rules engine's reason codes and the disclosure guidance, and the plan is written about "your client." Two convictions are likely erased once probation ends; one needs the year of the offense.
Demo 3 · Practice Room

A formerly incarcerated woman preps for a job interview

Anna was in prison for a 2014 drug conviction. Since then, she’s finished a food safety course and volunteered in a kitchen. She is ready for a job and uses the app to practice how to approach her history. For each answer, the safety guard checks it, then the role-play partner and coach run at the same time, then code checks the coach’s output. The run took about 30 seconds and cost about one-seventh of a cent ($0.0015).

Logged practice-only session, November 8, 2026 (session b7cde69c): two answers and a summary; 7 model calls, 9,218 tokens, about $0.0015.
Hiring manager, landlord, or social worker; friendly, neutral, or skeptical. The coach scores five skills and suggests a "Try saying..." rewrite. The role-play partner and coach never see her convictions. Code keeps the scores at 1 to 3 per skill and replaces any coaching that tells her to deny a record that isn't erased.

What keeps it low

  • Rules in plain Python: free
  • Cached prompt start: 45% of input at 1/10 the price
  • One questionnaire instead of a chat: 24–44 calls down to 6–11
  • Caps: 150,000 tokens per session, 2M a day
At scale: about $1.34 a month for 100 users and $134 a month for 10,000. Hosting and keeping the resource list current cost more than the AI.
Prices: 10 cents per million input tokens, 1 cent cached, 50 cents output. The honest trade-off: one inexpensive model means the verifier sends about a third of plans back once.

Impact

A record that no longer exists still costs people jobs, homes, and time with their children if they don’t know it’s gone. The research below shows what clearing a record is worth.
